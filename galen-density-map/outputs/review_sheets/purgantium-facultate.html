RENDERING 1

For the argument is no less addressed to them than to you—to men who had never before heard you concede that there exists [333] attraction in any existing thing at all, but who yesterday heard you admit that everything contained in the veins is drawn in common by the drug. For it would have been right for them not merely not to admire, or to praise at all, what was said, but to regard it as a betrayal of the positions he laid down from the beginning. For concerning the separation of urine and of yellow bile and black, and many other activities in the body, you produce a great many arguments on every occasion, conceding anything rather than that attraction of what is proper to itself is a certain natural activity belonging to each body; but now you have conceded not only that attraction exists, but even attraction of absolutely everything—which is a far greater admission than to speak of attraction of what is proper. For that, just as there is in animals a certain power expulsive of what is alien, so there is also a certain power attractive of what is proper to each existing thing, seems reasonable to all men—at least to those who are not yet wise with our wisdom; but that all things are drawn alike by something, whether they happen to be akin in their qualities or most contrary, is full of astonishing unreason.

RENDERING 2

For the argument is addressed no less to them than to you: to those who had never before heard you concede that attraction exists [333] in anything whatsoever, but yesterday heard you agreeing that all the contents of the veins alike are attracted by the drug. For it would have been right for them, far from admiring or in any way praising what was said, to regard it as a betrayal of what he had laid down from the beginning. For on every occasion you people hold a great many discourses about the separation of urine, and of yellow and black bile, and about many other activities in the body, conceding anything rather than that attraction is a natural activity belonging to each body with respect to what is proper to it. But now you have conceded not only that attraction exists, but that it is of all things without qualification, which is a far greater admission than to say 'of what is proper.' For that, just as there is in animals a certain faculty that excretes what is alien, so there is also in each of the things that exist one that attracts what is proper, seems reasonable to all men, at least to those not yet wise with our wisdom. But that all things are attracted equally by something, whether they happen to be akin in their qualities or most contrary, is full of astonishing irrationality.

RENDERING 1

[336] Perhaps, then, you will inquire whether there is any drug that draws out blood straightaway from the very beginning. There is, my excellent friend, but no one would use it. Why? you will perhaps say. Because this would be to slaughter the man, not to purge him. For purgatives are called purgatives by everyone for this very reason, that they render the blood clean of the aforementioned residues. Yet there is no physician so wise as to wish to evacuate all the blood and to leave the other humours behind in the body. Hence they reckon among the deleterious poisons all such drugs as produce an evacuation of blood through the veins in the stomach and the intestines. But according to your hypothesis not even these ought to evacuate blood from the beginning. As for the names of such drugs, even if I knew them with certainty, I would not disclose them in writing. But this alone it is safe for me to relate: what happened among the Thracian Bithynians when we were still children. A certain man found a herb of such a kind that whoever took some of it excreted blood and so perished.

RENDERING 1

[326] To put it briefly, what is called purgation is, according to their account, a change and alteration of all the humours in the veins, not an evacuation of one of them. But if this is what you want, let us now say to them that whether someone takes a drug that draws bile, or phlegm, or black bile, or serous moisture, all the humours from the veins are evacuated. And indeed, purging someone with such a drug would then differ in this respect only from evacuating the blood by cutting a vein, inasmuch as in phlebotomies it is excreted such as it was present before, whereas in purgations it is altered not only in colour but also in consistency. For the drug that draws watery humours makes everything in the veins fluid and thin, even if it happened to be very thick before; while that which draws black bile, conversely, makes everything thick and stagnant, even if before it was very fluid. And accordingly, our choice between phlebotomizing and purging will be made according to certain other indications, but will not be made according to the very kind of the things evacuated. And what are these other indications?
