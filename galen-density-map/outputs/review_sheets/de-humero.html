RENDERING 1

For the shoulder itself, when let go, together with [418] the upper arm, would settle the bones against one another; a simple bandaging would suffice, and the formation of callus would take only a few days. Earlier he said that, for the most part, when the collarbone is snapped right through its whole thickness, the part of the collarbone that comes from the sternum shifts to the upper region, while the part that comes from the acromion becomes lower. Now, however, he writes about the rarer mode, in which the part from the acromion becomes higher and the part next to the sternum lower; this he also says is easier to treat. When it (the shoulder) is let down, the part of the collarbone continuous with the acromion also settles down along with it. And thus, having come level with the other part, it receives the bandaging and the treatment.

RENDERING 2

For the shoulder itself, when it is let down, and [418] the upper arm would settle the bones against one another, and some slight bandaging would suffice, and the formation of callus would be a matter of a few days. Earlier he said that for the most part the part of the clavicle on the side of the sternum, whenever it is broken clean across through the whole of itself, shifts toward the upper region, while the part on the side of the acromion becomes lower. But now he writes about the rarer mode, according to which the part on the side of the acromion becomes higher and the part toward the sternum lower, which he also says is easier to cure. When it (the shoulder) is let down, the part of the clavicle too, as much of it as is continuous with the acromion, settles down together with it; and thus, having come level with the other part, it obtains both the bandaging and the treatment.

RENDERING 1

And the acromion becomes painless [400] within a few days, if it is bandaged properly. He indicates that the acromion is a conjunction, and he says also that it is a bone and that it binds together both of the parts mentioned. It is clear, therefore, that the acromion is a cartilaginous bone lying upon the conjunction of the clavicle and the scapula, having membranes growing out from it, by which the extremities of the bones are bound together. When it has been torn away, the clavicle too is accustomed to follow along with it, and for this reason restoration comes about when the clavicle is forced downwards, so that certain circular bendings of it are produced. Here indeed this forcing-down succeeds better in the younger; for the further age advances, the drier our bodies become.

RENDERING 2

The acromion [400] becomes painless within a few days, if it is bandaged properly. He indicates that the acromion is a junction, and he also says that it is a bone and that it binds together both of the parts mentioned. It is clear, then, that the acromion is a cartilaginous bone lying upon the junction of the collarbone and the shoulder blade, having membranes growing out from it, by which the ends of the bones are bound together. When it is torn away, the collarbone too usually follows along with it, and for this reason correction is effected by forcing the collarbone downwards, so that certain rounded curvatures of it are produced. In this case, indeed, this forcing down succeeds better in younger people; for the further age advances, the drier our bodies become.

RENDERING 1

[370] For some people are by nature more prone than others to having prominent joints ('exarthrous'); but one must look at the same parts of the patient himself, to see if the healthy one is unlike the ailing one. 'And this will be said correctly, but it involves a very great deal of misapprehension. For such reasons it is not enough to know this art by reasoning alone; one must also be conversant with it through practical familiarity.' He does not simply call every kind of ignorance 'misapprehension' (parasynesis), but only that which lies alongside knowledge and comes close to it. He himself will teach in what follows how it happens that those who sometimes compare the healthy part next to the affected one are deceived.

RENDERING 2

And this will be said rightly, but it carries a very great deal of misapprehension (παραξύνεσις). For such reasons, moreover, it is not sufficient to know this art by reasoning alone, but one must also be conversant with it through actual practice (ὁμιλίῃ ὁμιλέειν). He does not simply call every ignorance "misapprehension" (παρασύνεσις), but that which lies alongside and comes near to knowledge. And he himself will teach, in what follows, how it comes about that those who sometimes compare the adjacent (ἐφεξῆς) healthy part with the affected one are deceived.

RENDERING 1

[413] For the chest is continuous with itself and with the spine. Nearest to the joint of the shoulder the clavicle is mobile; for it is compelled to be frequently moved because of its conjunction with the acromion. Besides, when it is injured, the part attached to the chest escapes toward the upper side and is not at all willing to be forced toward the lower side. Nearest to the joint of the shoulder the collarbone is loose (?); for it is compelled to be frequently moved on account of its junction with the acromion. Besides, when it is injured, the part attached at the chest escapes upward, and is not very willing to be forced into the lower position. For it is naturally light, and the open space for it is greater above than below. But the shoulder and the parts attached to these are easily detached from the ribs and the chest, and for this reason it can be drawn both much higher and lower.

Καλῶς γὰρ Ὅμηρος καταμεμαθήκει ὅτι πάντων τῶν προβάτων βόες ὅτι μάλιστα πονέουσι ταύτην τὴν ὥρην καὶ βοῶν οἱ ἀρόται, ὅτι κατὰ τὸν χειμῶνα ἐργάζονται.

RENDERING 1

For it has been shown in the works on temperaments that the person who is thinner by nature is drier in temperament, and the stouter one moister. So in the thin the substance of the muscles, being taut, binds the articulation tight. In the stouter, insofar as the flesh is more abundant, it closes off the space around the joints [355]; but insofar as it is soft, it is easily turned aside, receiving in succession the heads of the bald (?) bones as they are displaced. So the factors bearing on dislocations are set against each other (?) in each of the two. If, however, someone well-fleshed by nature becomes at some time thinner than he was, the joint would both fall out more easily for him and fall back in again at that time than before. Likewise, if someone who is slender by nature should put on flesh, the joint would fall out for him only with difficulty and would be reduced with difficulty. As testimonies of this account he records what is observed in oxen, expressing it so clearly that it needs no exegesis, unless perhaps there is one or two expressions of that sort, which I too shall point out. 'For Homer had rightly observed that of all livestock oxen suffer most at this season, and of oxen the ploughing ones, because they work during the winter.'

RENDERING 2

For it has been shown in the books On Mixtures that the man who is naturally leaner is drier in mixture, while the thicker man is moister, so that in the lean the substance of the muscles, being tense, binds the articulation tightly, whereas in the thicker, insofar as the fleshy parts are more abundant, they do indeed shut off the space around the joints, [355] but insofar as they are soft they are easily pushed aside, receiving one after another the heads of the bald (φαλακρῶν) bones as they are displaced, so that what concerns dislocations is set upright (ἀνίστασθαι) for both alike. If, however, someone who is naturally well-fleshed becomes at some time leaner than his usual self, the joint would both dislocate more easily in him and be put back again more easily at that time than before, just as, if someone naturally thin were to become fleshy, it would dislocate in him only with difficulty and would be reduced with trouble; and as evidence of this argument he writes down the phenomena observed in oxen, explaining them clearly, so that they need no exegesis, except if there should be somewhere one or two expressions of such a kind, on which I too shall comment. For Homer had well observed that of all grazing beasts oxen suffer most at this season, and of oxen the plough-oxen, because they work during the winter.
