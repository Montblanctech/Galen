Ὁκόσοι δὲ βούλονται λεπτοὶ ἐόντες παχύτεροι γενέσθαι, τά τε ἅλλα ποιέειν τἀναντία ἐκείνοισι οἶσιν ἔφην καὶ νῆστιν μηδεμίην ταλαιπωρίην ποιέεσθαι.

RENDERING 1

[196] It would have been better for him to have said, in addition to this, not "to take a single meal" but "to eat little." For some of those who take a single meal consume far more than those who eat twice. And just as the bath, by thoroughly warming the body and especially its exterior, contributes to the distribution of nourishment and to the apposition of what has been distributed, by the same reasoning abstention from bathing assists the opposite effects. And sleeping on a hard bed, too, distresses, contracts and constricts the body, and for this reason hinders its increase in every part. By the same reasoning, walking about naked cools all the parts that lie beneath the skin, but drives the heat together into the depth and into the viscera, so that the foods are indeed concocted well, but their carriage in every direction is hindered. Those who, being thin, wish to become stouter, should both do the other things opposite to those I have mentioned and undertake no exertion while fasting.

RENDERING 2

[196] It would have been better to have said at this point (πρὸς αὐτῷ) not 'to eat once a day' but 'to eat little'. For some of those who eat once a day take in much more than those who eat twice. Just as the bath, by heating the body through, and especially its exterior, contributes to the distribution of nourishment and to the apposition of what has been distributed, by the same reasoning abstention from bathing assists the opposite effects. Sleeping on a hard bed also distresses, constricts and binds the body, and for this reason prevents its growth into every part. By the same reasoning, walking about naked cools all the parts beneath the skin and drives the warmth together into the depth and the viscera, so that the foods are concocted well but their conveyance in every direction is prevented. Those who, being thin, wish to become fatter should do the other things opposite to those I have mentioned, and should undertake no exertion while fasting.

RENDERING 1

[220] "Those who are seized by thirsts: for these one should take away from both their foods and their exertions, and they should drink their wine watery and as cold as possible." Here the distinguishing sign of hot natures has been stated in this way, but in the sixth book of the Epidemics it is stated in the following passage: "In a hot nature, cold water as drink; to keep still." For he himself, having named the krasis of the body, wrote next the regimen which those so disposed require, a regimen having cooling as its principal point. And within this principal point of the regimen the more efficacious measures are water-drinking and abstention from exercise. In the present passage, however, he named the "keeping still" differently, saying "and to take away from the exertions." And instead of "to drink water" he wrote "and they should drink their wine watery and as cold as possible"; and he seems to me to have given better advice here. For the watery and cold drink is not deprived of its cooling power, but it has also taken on the benefits that come from the wine.

RENDERING 2

[220] Those whom thirsts seize, for these one should take away from both the foods and the exertions, and let them drink wine that is watery and as cold as possible. Now here the distinguishing mark of hot natures has been stated in this way, but in the sixth book of the Epidemics in the following passage: "In a hot nature, cold water as drink, to rest." For having himself named the temperament of the body, he wrote next the regimen which those so disposed need, which has cooling as its chief point. And within this chief point of the regimen the more effective things are water-drinking and abstention from exercise. But in the passage before us he named "resting" by another term, saying "and to take away from the exertions"; and in place of "drinking water" he wrote "and let them drink wine that is watery and as cold as possible"; and he seems to me to advise better here. For a watery and cold drink is not deprived of its cooling effect, but has in addition acquired the benefits that come from wine.

Ὁκόσους γυμναζομένους μάλιστα διάῤῥοιαι λαμβάνουσι καὶ τὰ ὑποχωρήματα σιτώδεα καὶ ἄπεπτα, τουτέοισι τῶν τε [213] γυμνασίων ἀφαιρέειν, μὴ ἔλασσον τοῦ τρίτου μέρους καὶ τῶν σιτίων τοῖσιν ἡμίσεσι χρέεσθαι. δῆλον γὰρ δὴ ὅτι ἡ κοιλίη ξυνθάλπειν οὐ δύναται, ὥστε πέσσεσθαι τὸ πλῆ- θος τῶν εἰσιόντων σιτίων. ἔστω δὲ τουτέοισι τὰ σιτία ἄρτος ὡς ἐξοπτότατος ἐν οἴνῳ ἐντετριμμένος καὶ τὰ πό- ματα ὡς ἐλάχιστα καὶ ἀκρητέστατα, καὶ περιπάτοισι μὴ χρεέσθωσαν ἀπὸ τοῦ σιτίου. μονοσιτέειν δὲ χρὴ ὑπὸ τοῦτον τὸν χρόνον, οὕτω γὰρ ἂν μάλιστα ξυνθάλποιτο ἡ κοιλίη καὶ τῶν εἰσιόντων ἐπικρατέοι. γίνεται δὲ ὁ τρό- πος οὗτος τῆς διαῤῥοίης τῶν σωμάτων μάλιστα τοῖσι πυ- κνοσάρκοισιν, ὁκόταν ἀναγκάζηται ὁ ἄνθρωπος κρεηφαγέειν, τῆς φύσιος ὑπαρχούσης τοιαύτης. αἱ γὰρ φλέβες πυκνω- θεῖσαι οὐκ ἀντιλαμβάνονται τῶν σιτίων τῶν εἰσιόντων.

RENDERING 1

Those who, while exercising, are especially seized by diarrhoeas, and whose evacuations are food-like (σιτώδεα) and unconcocted: from these one must take away some of their [213] exercises, not less than a third part, and they should use half of their foods. For it is clear that the belly is unable to warm them thoroughly, so that the quantity of the foods entering is concocted. Let the foods for these be bread baked as thoroughly as possible, crumbled into wine, and let the drinks be as few as possible and as unmixed as possible; and let them not take walks after the meal. They should eat only once a day during this time, for in this way the belly would most of all be warmed thoroughly and would master what enters. This kind of diarrhoea of bodies occurs especially in those of dense flesh, whenever the person is compelled to eat meat while his nature is of this kind. For the veins, having been made dense, do not take hold of the foods that enter.

RENDERING 2

Those whom, while exercising, diarrhoeas especially seize, and whose stools are food-like and unconcocted, for these one must take away from the [213] exercises not less than a third part, and use half the amount of food. For it is clear indeed that the belly is not able to warm things through so that the quantity of the food that enters is concocted. Let the food for these be bread as thoroughly baked as possible, crumbled into wine, and let the drinks be as small as possible and as unmixed as possible, and let them not take walks after the meal. One must eat a single meal during this time, for thus the belly would be most warmed through and would master what enters. This mode of diarrhoea arises most of all in bodies that are dense-fleshed, whenever the man is compelled to eat meat, his nature being of such a kind. For the veins, having become dense, do not take up the food that enters.

Ὁκόσοισι μὲν ἀνεπιτήδειον ἀπεμέειν τὰ σιτία, ἢ ὁκόσοισιν αἱ κοιλίαι οὐκ εὐδιέξοδοί εἰσι, τουτέοισι πᾶσι ξυμφέρει πολλάκις τῆς ἡμέρης ἐσθίειν καὶ παντοδαποῖσι βρώμασι [208] χρέεσθαι καὶ ὄψοισι πάντας τρόπους ἐσκευασμένοισι καὶ οἴνους τε πίνειν δισσοὺς ἢ τρισσούς. ὁκόσοισι δὲ μὴ ἀνεμέουσι τὰ σιτία, ἢ καὶ κοιλίας ἔχουσιν ὑγρὰς, τουτέοισι πᾶσιν ἐναντίον τουτέου τοῦ τρόπου ξυμφέρει ποιέειν. Ἐπιτήδειον μὲν ἀνεμεῖν τὰ σιτία τοῖς ἤτοι διαῤῥύψαι βουλομένοις τὴν κοιλίαν, ἢ κένωσιν μετρίαν ποιήσασθαι τοῦ παντός. ἀξιοῖ δὲ τούτους ἐσθίειν τε πολλάκις καὶ ποικίλα καὶ οἴνους πλείονας πίνειν. ἔμπαλιν δὲ τοὺς μήτε ἐμεῖν βουλομένους, ὑγροτέρας τε τοῦ προσήκοντος ἔχοντας κοιλίας, ὡς ἐπισχέσεως δεῖσθαι, μήτε πο- λλάκις ἐσθίειν, μήτε πολυειδῆ σιτία τε καὶ οἴνους προσφέρεσθαι. τὸ μὲν γὰρ ἅπαξ φαγεῖν ἕν τι μονοειδὲς ἔνα τε πίνειν οἶνον εἰς τὸ κατα- σχεῖν αὐτὰ τὴν γαστέρα τε καὶ πέψαι συντελεῖ, τὰ δὲ ἐναντία πεφθῆναί τε δυσχερέστερα καὶ μεῖναι κατὰ τὴν γα- στέρα.

RENDERING 1

For all those for whom it is unsuitable (ἀνεπιτήδειον) to vomit up their food, or whose bellies do not have an easy passage, it is beneficial to eat many times in the day and to make use of all sorts of foods [208] and of dishes prepared in every manner, and to drink two or three wines. But for all those who do not vomit up their food, or who also have moist bellies, it is beneficial to do the opposite of this manner. It is suitable to vomit up one's food for those who wish either to scour out the belly or to produce a moderate evacuation of the whole body. He thinks it right that these should both eat often and eat varied things and drink several wines. Conversely, those who do not wish to vomit, and who have bellies moister than is proper, so that they need restraining, should neither eat often nor take foods and wines of many kinds. For eating once, some single uniform food, and drinking one wine contributes to the stomach's both retaining them and concocting them, whereas the opposite things are harder to be concocted and to remain in the stomach.

RENDERING 2

Those for whom it is suitable to vomit up their food, or whose bellies are not easy of passage: for all these it is beneficial to eat many times a day, to make use of all kinds of foods [208] and of relishes prepared in every manner, and to drink two or three wines. But those who do not vomit up their food, or who also have moist bellies: for all these it is beneficial to do the opposite of this manner. It is suitable to vomit up food for those who wish either to cleanse out the belly or to make a moderate evacuation of the whole. He thinks it right that these should eat often and eat varied things, and drink several wines. Conversely, those who do not wish to vomit and who have bellies moister than is proper, so as to require checking, should neither eat often nor take foods and wines of many kinds. For eating once some single uniform thing and drinking one wine contributes to the stomach's retaining them and concocting them, whereas the opposite things are both harder to concoct and harder to remain in the stomach.

Τίνα καλεῖ κατακλύσματα διὰ τῶν ἐφεξῆς ἐδήλωσεν αὐτός. τά τε ἀπὸ τῶν γαλάκτων εἰπὼν καὶ τὸ ἀπὸ τῶν ἐρεβίνθων ἑψημένον ὕδωρ ἅλμην τε καὶ θάλασσαν. ἕτερα γάρ ἐστι τούτων τὰ καθαίροντα φάρμακα κενωτικὰ τοῦ παν- τὸς σώματος ὄντα.

RENDERING 1

"One should use vomitings and clysters of the [198] belly as follows: vomit during the six winter months, for this period is more phlegmatic than the summer, and the diseases arise about the head and about this region above the diaphragm. But when there is the heat, use clysters. For the season is burning, and the body is more bilious, and heavinesses occur in the loins and in the knees, and heats arise, and gripings occur in the belly. One must therefore cool the body and draw downward, away from these regions, the matters that are rising upward." What he calls clysters (kataklysmata) he has himself made clear in what follows, mentioning those made from milks, and the water in which chickpeas have been boiled, and brine and sea water. For the purging drugs, which are evacuant of the whole body, are something different from these.

RENDERING 2

One must use vomits and the washings-out of the [198] belly in this way: to vomit during the six winter months; for this time is more phlegmatic than the summer one, and the diseases arise about the head and about this region above the diaphragm. But when the hot weather is present, to use the washings-out. For the season is burning, and the body is more bilious, and feelings of heaviness in the loins and in the knees and heats come about, and gripings occur in the belly. One must therefore cool the body and draw downward the things that are raised up, away from these regions. What he calls washings-out he himself made clear through what follows, when he mentioned those from milks and the water boiled from chickpeas, and brine and sea-water. For the purging drugs, being evacuative of the whole body, are different from these.

RENDERING 1

If, then, from the treatise On Temperaments you were to gather and learn scientifically that the proper temperament of old men is cold and dry, but that they possess an acquired [189] moistness on account of the generation of phlegmatic residues, and, passing from those books to the commentaries On Matters of Health, you were to learn in addition how one ought to regulate the diet of old men, the lack of distinction in the passage now before us will be quite clear to you. For neither ought human life to have been divided into two, nor ought the bodies of old men to have been called moist and soft, nor ought it to have been said that they are to be dieted on foods dry in temperament. However, in saying that the young have firm bodies and are dry in temperament, he has evidently made his statement about those in their prime alone, and has called these 'young'. He therefore left out both the age of children and that of those past their prime, and, as I maintain, also the most well-tempered age, which lies between children and those in their prime. And he is evidently himself suspicious of his own statement. For he did not say simply that older people must be dieted in the drier manner, but added: 'for the greater part of the time'.
