τῆς δ’ ἑτέρας οὖν ζητήσεως [227] ἐκτετελεσμένης, ἐπὶ ταύτην οὖν λοιπὸν τραποίμην καὶ ἐπιχειρήσαιμι ἀνασκοπεῖσθαι, εἰ ὀρθῶς λέλεκται, πλεῖστον εἶναι τοῖς αὐξανομένοις τὸ θερμόν. κατὰ δὴ τὸ ἐξετάζειν ἕτερον θερμὸν, τριττὰ τυγχάνει ὄντα τὰ ἐπιζητήσεως δεόμενα καὶ μάλα πολὺ κεχωρισμένα ἀλλήλων. ἓν μὲν τί ποτέ ἐστι τὸ πλεῖον ἄλλο ἄλλου θερμὸν, ἕτερον δὲ τί ποτέ ἐστιν ἄλλο ἄλλου θερμότερον, καὶ τρίτον τί ποτέ ἐστιν ἄλλο ἄλλου θερμὸν ἰσχυρότερον. εἰ μὲν οὖν τύχῃ τις ἐρόμενος ῾μᾶς ποῖόν τί ἐστι τὸ πλεῖον ἄλλο ἄλλου θερμὸν, τὸ ἀνάλογον ὀφείλομεν ἀποκρίνεσθαι ὡς καὶ πρὸς τὸν ἕτερον ἐρόμενον ποῖόν τί ἐστιν ἕτερον ἑτέρου ὑγρὸν πλεῖον ἀποκρινοίμεθα. ὥσπερ γὰρ ἐνταῦθα πρῶτον τὸ ἐπερώτημα πρὸς τὸ εἰρημένον ἐστὶν, οὐκέτι ἄλλο τι ὀρθῶς ἀποκρίνεσθαι ἀλλ’ ἢ μόνον τοῦτο τὸ τῇ οὐσίᾳ ὑπερβάλλειν πλεῖον, ἀμέλει ὑγρὸν ἄν τις εἴποι τὸν ἀμφορέα τοῦ ἡμίσεως.

RENDERING 1

The other inquiry, then, having been [227] completed, I would now turn to this remaining one and attempt to examine whether it has been rightly said that heat is greatest in growing bodies. Now in examining one heat against another, there happen to be three things that require inquiry, and these very far separated from one another: one, what it is for one heat to be more than another; another, what it is for one thing to be hotter than another; and the third, what it is for one heat to be stronger than another. If, then, someone should happen to ask us of what sort is the heat that is more than another, we ought to answer analogously to what we would answer to another who asked of what sort is one moist thing that is more than another. For just as here, the question being directed in the first place to what was said, it is no longer correct to answer anything else than only this, that what exceeds in substance is more: one would, of course, say that the amphora [holds] more liquid than the half.

RENDERING 2

Now that the first inquiry [227] has been brought to completion, I may turn next to this one and attempt to examine whether it has been rightly said that the heat is greatest in those who are growing. In examining one heat against another, the things that require inquiry happen to be three, and very widely separated from one another: one, what it is for one thing to be more heat than another; a second, what it is for one thing to be hotter than another; and a third, what it is for one thing to be a stronger heat than another. If, then, someone should happen to ask us what it is for one thing to be more heat than another, we ought to give the answer analogous to the one we would give to the other man who asks what it is for one thing to be more moisture than another. For just as there, once the question is put in the first place with reference to what has been stated, it is no longer correct to answer anything else except this alone, that it exceeds in substance, being more: one would doubtless say that the amphora is more moisture than the half-amphora.

οὕτως δὲ καὶ τὸ σπέρμα συγκείμενον ἔκ τε τῆς ὀῤῥώδους ὑγρότητος καὶ τοῦ πνεύματος τοῦ κατ’ αὐτὴν, ἐλάχιστον μὲν ἐν τοῖς γεγηρακόσι, πλεῖστον δ’ ἐστὶν ἐν τοῖς τῶν αὐξανομένων σώμασι, καὶ τούτων μάλιστα [239] ἐν τοῖς νεωτάτοις, ὡς ἐν τοῖς περὶ σπέρματος ὑπομνήμασιν ἀπεδείξαμεν. εἰ γοῦν ἀπορήσεις, εἰ ῥᾳδίως ἀναλίσκεται καὶ διαῤῥεῖ τὸ σῶμα τῶν αὐξανομένων; καὶ μὴν ἐπὶ γε τῶν ἄλλων οὐκ ἀγνοεῖς ὕδωρ μὲν θερμὸν ἢ ἔλαιον ἤ τι τῶν οὕτως ὑγρῶν ἐλαχίστῳ διαφορούμενον ὅλον. οὔτε γὰρ γῆν οὔτε λίθον οὔτ’ ἄλλο τῶν ψυχρῶν φύσει καὶ ξηρῶν. δύο μὲν γάρ εἰσιν ἐν ἅπασι τοῖς οὖσιν ἐπιτηδειόταται ποιότητες εἰς διαφόρησιν, ὑγρότης καὶ θερμότης, δύο δὲ ἔμπαλιν εἰς διαμονὴν, ξηρότης καὶ ψυχρότης, ὥστ’ οὐδὲν θαυμαστὸν, εἰ τὰ μὲν τῶν παιδίων σώματα διά τε τὴν ὑγρότητα καὶ θερμότητα ῥᾳδίως διαπνεῖται, τὰ δὲ τῶν γερόντων οὐ ῥᾳδίως ὑπὸ ξηρότητος καὶ ψύξεως πεπηγυῖαν ἐχόντων τὴν οὐσίαν δὲ ὥστ’, ὦ βέλτιστε Λύκε, περὶ ὧν ἐχρῆν ἐπιτιμηθῆναί σε, περὶ τούτων ἐγκαλεῖς. ἀγνοῶν οὖν ἐφάνης ἕν τι τῶν πρώτων μαθημάτων, ὃ μηδεὶς τῶν εἰσηγμένων νομίμως ἀγνοεῖ.

RENDERING 1

So too the seed, composed of the serous moisture and of the pneuma that is in it, is least in those who have grown old and greatest in the bodies of those who are growing, and among these especially [239] in the youngest, as we demonstrated in the commentaries On Seed. If, at any rate, you will raise the difficulty whether the body of those who are growing is easily consumed and flows away: well, in the case of other things, at least, you are not unaware that hot water or oil or any of the fluids of this kind is wholly dispersed in the shortest time, whereas neither earth nor stone nor any other of the things cold and dry by nature is. For in all things that exist there are two qualities most suited for dispersal, moisture and heat, and conversely two for permanence, dryness and coldness; so that it is no wonder if the bodies of children, on account of their moisture and heat, are easily transpired, while those of the old are not easily so, since they have a substance congealed by dryness and cooling. So that, my excellent Lycus, you bring charges concerning the very things for which you ought to have been censured. You have shown yourself, then, to be ignorant of one of the first lessons, which none of those who have been properly introduced is ignorant of.

RENDERING 2

So too the semen, composed of the serous moisture and the pneuma within it, is least in the aged and greatest in the bodies of those who are growing, and of these most of all [239] in the youngest, as we demonstrated in the commentaries On Semen. If, then, you raise the difficulty whether the body of those who are growing is easily consumed and flows away? Yet in the case of other things you are not unaware that hot water or oil or any of the things that are moist in this way is wholly dispersed in the shortest time. For neither earth nor stone nor any other of the things cold and dry by nature is so. For in all things that exist there are two qualities most apt for dispersion, moisture and heat, and conversely two for persistence, dryness and coldness; so that it is no wonder if the bodies of children, because of their moisture and heat, are easily transpired, while those of old men are not easily so, since they have their substance congealed by dryness and cold. So, most excellent Lycus, you bring charges about the very things for which you yourself ought to have been rebuked. You have been shown, then, to be ignorant of one of the first lessons, of which none of those who have been properly introduced is ignorant.

RENDERING 1

It is astonishing, then, if someone perceives [205] the difference in the case of smoke and flame, but in the case of our bodies is insensible of a heat that is sometimes very strong, like that of flame, and sometimes distressing and biting, like that of smoke. And I am no less astonished if he does not concede that the ill-mixtures in respect of heat in animals are analogous to those in baths and houses; as, then, in houses the air of a smoky house is one thing, and that of a simply hot house another, in the same way, I think, evidently different from these is that air in which there is a multitude of burning lamps or of torch-wood together with soot, and accordingly we also call this sooty, just as we call the other air smoky, since it arises from the over-roasting of material. For soot differs from smoke in this respect—although both are residues of flame—that soot is an earthy exhalation of material that has been roasted through, whereas smoke is neither of over-roasted material nor earthy, but of material that is half-burnt and mixed from a moist and an earthy substance.

RENDERING 2

It is surprising, then, if someone perceives [205] the difference in the case of smoke and flame, yet in the case of our own bodies is insensible that the warmth is sometimes most intense, like that of flame, and sometimes distressing and biting, like that of smoke. And I am no less surprised if he does not concede that the dyscrasias with respect to heat in animals are analogous to those in baths and houses. For just as in houses the air of a smoky house is one thing and that of a simply hot house another, in the same way, I think, distinct from these is clearly the air in which there is a multitude of burning lamps, or a torch together with soot; and accordingly we call this air, just as we call the other smoky, sooty, since it arises from the over-roasting of material. For soot differs from smoke in this respect, although both are residues of flame: soot is an earthy exhalation of thoroughly roasted material, while smoke is neither of over-roasted material nor earthy, but of half-burnt material, and mixed from a moist and an earthy substance.

RENDERING 1

If, on the other hand, he says that it is more in growing bodies and that this warmth is intensified, perhaps [meaning] hotter than that, as one might speak who wished to say that snow is whiter than some other white thing, [meaning] not that it is whiter or more white, but that its white is more than this white, then Hippocrates will seem to err in both respects, both in not using the word well and in saying something false besides. He would seem to err in not using the [232] word well, because he placed this word, which is applied to more substance, wishing to signify that a quality more intensified in warmth is present. And he would seem to have said something false, because the heat in growing bodies is not most intensified in quality, not as it should be, hotter in this age than in any other. But he said that it is most intensified in growing bodies; and that this is false, that heat is most intensified in growing bodies, it is easy for anyone to recognise. For no sign would be found present in the body of growing persons of a greatest intensification of heat in warmth. For what was said by him in the treatise On the Nature of Man, as evidence that heat is most in growing bodies, is altogether foolish. For [he says] that he declares man to be hotter in the first period of life, inferring this from the growth that is then accomplished in the greatest measure.

RENDERING 2

But if, on the other hand, he says that those who are growing have more [heat] and [means] this warming as intensified, presumably hotter than the other, as if someone wishing to say that snow is whiter than some other white thing should say not that it is whiter or more white, but that the white is more than this white, then Hippocrates will seem to err on both counts, both in not using the expression well and, apart from that, in saying something false. In not using the [232] expression well he would seem to go wrong because he applied this expression, which is assigned to what is greater in substance, wishing to signify that the quality is more intensified in respect of warming. And he would seem to have stated a falsehood, because the heat in those who are growing is not most intensified in respect of quality, nor is the heat, as it should be [for his claim], hotter in this age than in some other. But he said that it is most intensified in those who are growing; and that this is in fact false, that the heat is most intensified in those who are growing, is easy for anyone to establish. For no sign existing in the body would be found in those who are growing of the heat being most intensified in respect of warming. For what is said by him in the treatise On the Nature of Man, as a proof that heat is greatest in those who are growing, is utterly foolish: namely, to declare that a human being is hotter in the first period of life, inferring this from the fact that growth is then accomplished to the greatest degree.
