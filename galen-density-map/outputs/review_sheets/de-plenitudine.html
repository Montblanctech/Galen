RENDERING 1

And the very symptoms of them that have now been mentioned, apart from the bad colour, belong to them when they have occupied the brain and the stomachos [577]; for sleeplessness and sleep come about on account of the brain, while appetites and losses of appetite come about on account of the stomachos, which is disposed differently at different times. And it is clear that the mouth of the stomach has here been called stomachos, in which the phlegm that has gathered, as was said a little earlier, produced the appetites; but of the bilious humours, in the case of the yellow they will be thirsty and nauseous, and their mouth is bitter; whereas in the case of the black, they are despondent, sullen, fearful, without thirst, and full of saliva, but not all of them averse to food, only some of them. And those are considerably hungry in whom the black bile has the acid quality predominating; and this comes about when it is rendered more malignant. But so long as it is merely like a sediment of blood, and not yet precisely acid, it is perhaps better (so far as it is called a melancholic humour) not yet to call such a humour black bile either, but, as I am accustomed, melancholic, because it has come near to becoming black bile.

RENDERING 2

And these very symptoms of theirs just now stated, except for the loss of colour, belong to [humours] that have taken possession of the brain and the stomach [577]; for sleeplessness and sleep come about on account of the brain, and appetites and want of appetite on account of the stomach, as it is disposed now in one way, now in another. It is clear that by 'stomach' (stomachos) is now meant the mouth of the belly (gastēr), in which the phlegm that has collected, as was said a little before, produced the appetites. Of the bilious, those with yellow bile will be thirsty and nauseated, and their mouth is bitter; those with black bile are despondent, sullen, fearful, free of thirst and full of saliva; not all of them are averse to food, but some of them are. Those in whom the black [bile] has the sour quality predominating are considerably hungry; and this comes about when it is rendered more malignant. But so long as it is only like a sediment of blood, and not yet strictly sour, it is perhaps better (until it is called melancholic humour) not yet to call such a humour black bile at all, but, as I am accustomed, melancholic, because it has come close to becoming black bile.

RENDERING 1

But now is not the occasion to go through the difference among the cacochymiai, since I said that they are not even called plethos; for our proposed task was not simply to discover what is in excess in whatever manner, but to inquire about plethos; [578] and this is an excess of the humours throughout the whole body of the animal. One ought not, then, to desire to hear in this discourse about the forms either of the local swellings or of cacochymia, but about when either all the humours are increased according to their original proportion, or one of them acquires some slight preponderance. Now blood can acquire a very great preponderance in the plethoric dispositions, but none of the other humours can; for such a thing is already cacochymia, and is no longer called plethos, nor plethora.

RENDERING 2

But this is not the time to go through the differences among the cacochymias, since I said that they are not even called plenitude. Our proposed task was not simply to find out whatever is in excess in any way whatsoever, but to examine plenitude [578]; and this is an excess of the humours throughout the whole body of the animal. So in this discourse one must not long to hear the kinds either of swellings in particular parts or of cacochymia, but only of the case when either all the humours have increased in their original proportion, or one of them has acquired some slight preponderance. Now blood can acquire a very great preponderance in plethoric conditions, but none of the other humours can; for such a thing is by then called cacochymia, and no longer plenitude or plethora.

RENDERING 1

And indeed there is also a third, further band, which neither knows whether there is or is not some faculty, nor admits to inquiring into it, but asserts that, on the basis of certain clearly apparent signs which come together in the patient, the evacuation has been discovered by itself from long experience; then, when asked what exactly [516] these signs are, it says distension of the vessels, and redness and heaviness of the whole body, and reluctance toward movements, and tensions of the limbs. Some of them add also the ulcerous and lassitude-like sensation, and an idle previous manner of life amid rather abundant intakes of foods and drinks, and the suppression of customary excretions. Upon this syndrome, at any rate—for so they call the aggregate of symptoms—phlebotomy has been observed to be beneficial. These men, then, deprive not only themselves but also all other human beings of reason, asserting that everything has been discovered from experience; while the others, as has just now been said, say that they use reason, and as for the faculty, some say that it does not exist at all, others that it exists, but that what its substance is is not known; so that very few are those who dare to declare both the existence and the substance of a faculty.
