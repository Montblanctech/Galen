RENDERING 1

For we shall have that one included in the other part of the dense breath spoken of at the beginning, since it is the same as the dense and [928] large; and the rare and small, in turn, in the cold, for this one too does not differ from the cold. Why, then, did he write it not as rare and small but as cold? Because, first, he wished to indicate it from the more readily apparent symptom; second, to point out the cause on account of which he calls it deadly; and third, because he supposed that in this way we would most quickly understand both that this very one differs in no way from the rare and small, and that its opposite, the hot, differs in no way from the dense and large. For the cold breath is in every case both rare and small, as has also been shown in the second book; and as for the dense and large, that which is indicative of burning heat (phlogosis) of the respiratory organs, no one would even rightly speak of it as such unless it were also breathed out hot. And indeed, by adding, to the cold breath alone, that it 'now becomes exceedingly deadly', he clearly shows the opinion he holds concerning the three dyspnoeas spoken of before. For if he considered any of those too to be deadly, he would surely have added this somewhere. Did he then suppose them to be free of danger? By no means; rather, not deadly, yet not for that reason free of danger either.

RENDERING 2

For we shall find that one comprised in one of the two parts of the dense breathing spoken of at the beginning, since it is the same as the dense and [928] large; and the sparse and small, in turn, in the cold, since this too does not differ from the cold. Why then did he write of it not as sparse and small but as cold? Because, first, he wished to indicate it by the more readily apparent symptom; second, he wished to point to the cause on account of which he calls it deadly; and third, because he supposed that in this way we would most quickly understand both that this very breathing differs in no way from the sparse and small, and that its opposite, the hot, differs in no way from the dense and large. For cold breathing is in every case sparse and small, as was also shown in the second book; and the dense and large breathing, which is indicative of fiery heat (phlogōsis) of the respiratory organs, one could not even rightly call so unless it were also exhaled hot. Moreover, by adding to the cold breathing alone that it 'is then very deadly', he clearly shows what opinion he holds about the three difficulties of breathing spoken of before. For if he had considered any of those also deadly, he would surely have added it somewhere. Did he then suppose them to be without danger? By no means; rather, not deadly, but no longer without danger either.

RENDERING 1

But it was not in accordance with the delirium [957] that he breathed with difficulty; for the greater condition, the one in the hypochondrium, by itself stamped the form of the respiration, while the lesser one was overcome. So much for what is in the fifth book. Passing again to the sixth, of which we have already earlier explained the passage it shares with the second, let us come to the remaining ones. And almost all of them are by now clear because of what has been said before. For when he writes 'breath' alone, simply in this way, in the concurrences of diseases, he has been shown to use it in place of the dyspnoea from which the breath becomes frequent; and this same thing, when it becomes frequent to a greater degree, it is his custom to call asthma. So that in the sixth book of the Epidemics, when he says 'breath is left behind within', we shall understand the dyspnoea accompanied by frequency. Likewise also in the case of the woman in Crannon, of whom he writes thus: 'spleen naturally large, burning fever, very red, breath' — the aforesaid dyspnoea is to be understood, in which the breath becomes frequent, as also in the case of the daughter of Agisis.

RENDERING 2

But it was not in accordance with the delirium [957] that he had difficulty of breathing; for the greater affection, the one in the hypochondrium, stamped the form of the respiration in accordance with itself, while the lesser was overcome. These, then, are the cases in the fifth book. Passing over once more to the sixth, of which we have already explained earlier the passage it shares with the second, let us come to the remaining passages. Nearly all of them are by now clear because of what has been said before. For it was shown that when, in the concurrences of symptoms of diseases, he writes simply 'breath' (pneuma) on its own in this way, he means the difficulty of breathing from which the breath becomes rapid; and this same thing, when it is made rapid to a still greater degree, it is his custom to call panting (asthma). Likewise also in the case of the woman in Crannon, about whom he writes thus: 'spleen naturally large, ardent fever, very red, breath', we must understand the aforementioned difficulty of breathing, in which the breath is made rapid, as also in the case of the daughter of Agisias.

τὰς δ’ ἄλλας αἰτίας [911] τούτων ἐν τῷ πρώτῳ γράμματι διήλθομεν, ὥσπέρ γε καὶ ὅτι θερμασίας πολλῆς ἐν τοῖς ἀναπνευστικοῖς ὀργάνοις ἠθροισμένης, πυκνὸν καὶ μέγα γενήσεται τὸ πνεῦμα. φαίνεται δὲ καὶ τοῦτο σχεδὸν ἁπάσης ἡμέρας ἐπὶ τῶν διακαῶν πάντων πυρετῶν, ὥστ’ οὐδ’ αὐτὸ διαλάθοι τινά. καὶ μέν τοι καὶ ὡς τὴν παρὰ φύσιν φλόγωσιν οὐχ Ἱπποκράτης μόνον, ἀλλὰ καὶ οἱ ἄλλοι σχεδὸν ἅπαντες οἱ πρὸ Ἐρασιστράτου φλεγμονὴν ὠνόμαζον, οὐχ ἡμῖν μόνοις, ἀλλὰ καὶ σύμπασιν ὡς εἰπεῖν τοῖς Ἱπποκράτους ἐξηγηταῖς ἀποδέδεικται. τούτων οὖν ὑποκειμένων, οὐδὲν ἔτι θαυμαστὸν ἑνὶ κεφαλαίῳ τὰς εἰρημένας ἀμφοτέρας διαφορὰς δηλῶσαι τὸν Ἱπποκράτην, πνεῦμα δὲ πυκνὸν μὲν ἐὸν, εἰπόντα, πόνον σημαίνει ἢ φλεγμονὴν ἐν τοῖσιν ὑπὲρ τῶν φρενῶν χωρίοισιν, ὡς ἂν καὶ ἡμῶν νοῆσαι δυναμένων, ὅτι τὸ μὲν μετὰ μικρότητος πόνον σημαίνει, τὸ δὲ μετὰ μεγέθους φλεγμονὴν, ὅ πέρ ἐστι φλόγωσιν, δηλοῖ· προσθέντα δ’ εὐλόγως, ἐν τοῖσιν ὑπὲρ τῶν φρενῶν χωρίοισιν, ἤτοι τοῖς ἀναπνευστικοῖς, ὡς ἂν τῶν κάτω τοῦ διαφράγματος ἀμέτρων θερμασιῶν εἰς μέγεθός τε καὶ πυκνότητα τὴν ἀναπνοὴν ἐξᾶραι μὴ δυναμένων.

RENDERING 1

The other causes [911] of these we went through in the first book, just as we also showed that, when much heat has accumulated in the respiratory organs, the breath will become frequent and large. This too is evident almost every day in all burning fevers, so that it could not escape anyone's notice either. Moreover, not only we ourselves but practically all the commentators on Hippocrates have demonstrated that not only Hippocrates but also almost all the others before Erasistratus called burning heat (phlogōsis) contrary to nature 'phlegmonē'. These things being laid down, then, it is no longer surprising that Hippocrates indicated both of the aforementioned differences under a single heading, when he said: 'breath, being frequent, signifies pain or phlegmonē in the regions above the phrenes'. He said this on the assumption that we too are able to understand that the frequent breath with smallness signifies pain, whereas that with largeness indicates phlegmonē, which is to say phlogōsis. He reasonably added 'in the regions above the phrenes', that is, in the respiratory parts, since immoderate heats below the diaphragm are unable to raise the breathing to largeness and frequency.

RENDERING 2

The other causes [911] of these we went through in the first book, just as we also showed that, when much heat has been gathered in the respiratory organs, the breath will become frequent and large. And this too is seen almost every day in all burning fevers, so that it too could not escape anyone's notice. Moreover, that not Hippocrates alone but also nearly all the others before Erasistratus called the unnatural burning (phlogosis) 'phlegmone' has been demonstrated not by us alone but by practically all the exegetes of Hippocrates. These things, then, being laid down, it is no longer at all surprising that Hippocrates indicated both the aforesaid differentiae under a single heading, saying 'breath, when it is frequent, signifies pain or phlegmone in the regions above the phrenes', since we too are able to understand that the breath that is frequent together with smallness signifies pain, whereas that which is frequent together with largeness indicates phlegmone, that is, phlogosis; and that he reasonably added 'in the regions above the phrenes', that is, the respiratory regions, since immoderate heats below the diaphragm are unable to raise the respiration to largeness and frequency.

RENDERING 1

And moreover, after this woman, having gone on not a little way, [953] he begins to narrate in the following manner: 'The wife of Timaenes, pneumatic (πνευματώδης).' For this can be understood as dyspnoeic, and it can also be taken, as most of the commentators think, as meaning that she had her hypochondria filled with pneuma; but it seems to me better, and perhaps even necessary, that it be the woman whose hypochondria were inflated with pneuma who is meant, rather than a dyspnoeic one. Then, having gone on a little in the account, he says, 'and in some degree breathing more easily,' as though he had already said beforehand of her that she was dyspnoeic. And that he is accustomed to call 'pneumatic' those whose breathing is frequent has been stated in what precedes, but it is clear now as well; for the symptoms of this woman turned once more for the worse, and he says that she died on the sixteenth day with her breathing frequent.

RENDERING 2

And indeed after this one, having gone on not a little further, [953] he begins his account in this manner: 'The woman of Timaenes, full of pneuma.' For this can be understood as 'dyspnoeic', but it can also be taken to be said, as most of the commentators think, of one inflated with pneuma in the hypochondria. Yet it seems to me better, and perhaps necessary, that she be said to be inflated in the hypochondria rather than dyspnoeic. Having gone on a little further in the account, then, he says 'and somewhat easier of breathing', as though he had already said of her that she was dyspnoeic. That he is accustomed to call 'full of pneuma' those whose breathing is frequent has been stated in what precedes as well, but it is clear now too: for the symptoms of this woman had turned again for the worse, and he says that she died on the sixteenth day, having frequent breathing.

RENDERING 1

So that, of the four combinations which he wrote down in the Epidemics, the first two have been stated without distinction, [907] unclearly and confusedly, and only the third, as we said, has been written clearly, both itself and what is indicated by it, the two alike. And not only in these points would his account seem to be unclear and at the same time lacking in distinctions and to have left out the greater part, but also from what he says next: 'but breath exhaled cold from the nostrils and the mouth is already very deadly.' For why ever, if he had resolved to go through the differences relating to hot and cold, did he mention only the cold one? And further, why ever, in the case of the other two, the one which he calls without distinction 'frequent breath' and the one which, drawing a distinction, he calls 'sparse and large', did he note down only the conditions themselves, but did not indicate in what they will end, saying that pain and inflammation are indicated by the frequent difficulty of breathing, and delirium by the one that is sparse and at the same time large?

RENDERING 2

So that, of the four conjugations which he wrote in the Epidemics, the first two have been stated indeterminately [907] and unclearly and confusedly, and only the third, as we said—both it itself and what is signified by it—has been written clearly. And not only in these matters would the account seem to be unclear and at the same time undetermined and to leave out the greater part, but also from what he says in what follows: "But breath exhaled cold from the nostrils and the mouth is by now very deadly." For why on earth, if he had decided to go through the differences with respect to hot and cold, did he mention the cold one alone? And further, why on earth, in the case of the other two—the one which he names indeterminately "frequent breath" and the one which, making a distinction, he names "rare and large"—did he write down only the conditions themselves, but did not indicate in what they will end, saying that pain and inflammation are signified by the frequent dyspnoea, and delirium by the dyspnoea that is at once rare and large?
