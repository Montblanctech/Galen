κᾂν μὲν τοῦθ’ οὕτως ἔχῃ, θαυμαστὸν οὐδὲν, εἰ μὴ μόνον τοῖς ληθαργικοῖς, ἀλλὰ καὶ τοῖς φρενιτικοῖς [655] συμπίπτῃ. τὸ δ’ οὐχ ὑπνῶδες κῶμα τούτοις μὲν οὐκ ἔστιν ἴδιον, γίνεται δ’ ἐνίοτε. εὑρήσεις γὰρ πολλοὺς φρενιτικοὺς οὔτε ἀνισταμένους οὔτε ἐπαίρειν τοὺς ὀφθαλμοὺς ὅλως δυναμένους, ἀλλ’ ἐπὶ τοῦ αὐτοῦ μένοντας ὁμοίους τοῖς ληθαργικοῖς, ὥστε τούτους οἱ πολλοὶ καὶ ἀπαίδευτοι τῶν ἰατρῶν, οὔτε τί δεῖ καλεῖν οἴδασιν, ἀλλὰ παντάπασιν ἀποροῦσι, καὶ ἀλλότριον δοκεῖ αὐτοῖς εἶναι τοῦτο τὸ πάθος καὶ ἀνώνυμον, ἔνιοι δὲ σύμμικτόν ἐκ ληθάργου τε καὶ φρενίτιδος ὑπολαμβάνουσι, καὶ προσαγορεύουσι τυφομανίαν. καὶ τὸ κάκιστον πάντων, τινὲς τῶν Ἱπποκρατείων εἶναι προσποιουμένων τοῦτό φασι, καίτοι δήπου σαφῶς οὕτω καὶ φανερῶς γράφοντος Ἱπποκράτους, οὐκ ἐν ἀμφιδόξῳ βιβλίῳ, ἀλλ’ εἴπερ τις ἄλλος τῶν γνησίων εἶναι πεπιστευμένῳ τῷ τρίτῳ τῶν ἐπιδημιῶν, οὔτ’ ἐξεμάνη τῶν φρενιτικῶν οὐδεὶς, ὥσπερ ἐπ’ ἄλλοισιν, ἀλλ’ ἄλλῃ τινὶ καταφορῇ νωθρῇ βαρέως ἀπώλλυντο. οὐ μόνον γὰρ ὅτι καταφορὰ ταῖς φρενίτισιν ἐνίοτε συμπίπτει διήγγειλεν, ἀλλ’ ὅτι καὶ νωθρὰ πολλάκις. τίς δ’ ἡ νωθρά;

RENDERING 1

And if this is so, it is nothing remarkable if it befalls not only lethargics but also phrenitics. [655] The non-somnolent coma, however, is not peculiar to these latter, but it does occur at times. For you will find many phrenitics who neither get up nor are able to raise their eyes at all, but remain in the same position like lethargics, so that the common run of uneducated physicians do not know what these ought to be called, but are altogether at a loss, and this affection seems to them to be something foreign and nameless, while some suppose it to be a mixture compounded of lethargy and phrenitis, and name it typhomania. And, worst of all, some of those who pretend to be Hippocrateans say this, although Hippocrates surely writes so clearly and manifestly, not in a book of disputed authenticity but in one which, if any other, is believed to be among the genuine works, the third book of the Epidemics: "nor did any of the phrenitics go raving mad, as in other cases, but they perished heavily with some other sluggish sinking (kataphora)." For he announced not only that sinking sometimes befalls cases of phrenitis, but also that it is often sluggish. And what is the sluggish one?

RENDERING 2

And if this is so, it is no wonder if [coma] befalls not only lethargic patients but also phrenitic [655] patients. The coma that is not sleepy, however, is not proper to the latter, though it does occur sometimes. For you will find many phrenitics who neither get up nor are able to raise their eyes at all, but remain in the same position, like lethargic patients. So the majority of physicians, the uneducated ones, do not know what they ought to call these patients; they are entirely at a loss, and this affection seems to them foreign and nameless. Some suppose it to be a mixture of lethargy and phrenitis, and call it typhomania. And worst of all, some of those who profess to be Hippocrateans say this, although surely Hippocrates writes thus clearly and plainly, not in a book of disputed authenticity but in the third book of the Epidemics, which is believed, if any other is, to be among the genuine ones: 'nor did any of the phrenitics go raving mad, as in other cases, but they perished grievously in some other sluggish torpor.' For he reported not only that torpor sometimes befalls cases of phrenitis, but also that it is often sluggish. And what is the sluggish one?

παραπλήσιον γάρ τι συμβαίνει πολλῶν ἀρχομένοις νοσημάτων, οἷον ἔν τισι [664] τῶν μεθυσθέντων ἔστιν ἰδεῖν γιγνόμενον. ὅταν μὲν κωματώδεις ὦσι, καὶ σφάλλονται, καὶ βαρῶσι τὴν κεφαλὴν, καὶ μηδ’ εἰ βιάζοιντο διαίρειν δύνανται τὰ βλέφαρα· δι’ αὐτὸ δ’ αὖ πάλιν τοῦτο τὸ βάρος ὑπνοῦν ἀδυνατοῦσιν ἀσώμενοι, καὶ στρεφόμενοι, καὶ ῥιπτοῦντες ἑαυτοὺς, καὶ μεταβάλλοντες ἄλλοτ’ εἰς ἄλλο σχῆμα. ταῦτα γὰρ ἔστιν ἰδεῖν πολλάκις ἐπὶ τῶν μεθυσθέντων γιγνόμενα, πληρωθείσης αὐτῶν ἐν τῇ μέθῃ τῆς κεφαλῆς, εἶθ’ ὑπὸ τοῦ βάρους ἅμα τ’ εἰς κῶμα κατασπώσης ἅμα τε ὕπνον κωλυούσης. οὕτως οὖν καὶ πλήθους ἀπέπτου τὴν κεφαλὴν κατειληφότος, ἄγρυπνοί τε ἅμα καὶ κωματώδεις εἰσίν. καὶ τοῦτο κατ’ ἀρχὰς γενόμενον, ἔστιν ὅτε μὲν πεφθῇ καλῶς, ὥσπερ καὶ ἐξ οἴνου τοιοῦτο εἴη γεγονός· ἀλλ’ οὕτω μὲν οὔτε λήθαργος, οὔτε φρενῖτις ἠκολούθησεν· ἔστιν ὅτε δὲ δριμὺ γενόμενον εἰς φρενίτιδα ἐτελεύτησε. πολλοῖς δ’ αὖ τὸ μὲν λεπτότερον ἢ πεφθὲν ἢ διαφορηθὲν ἐκενώθη, ὑπέμεινε δὲ τὸ παχύτερον, καὶ οὕτω λήθαργος ἐπεγένετο.

RENDERING 1

For something similar happens to those at the beginning of many diseases, such as one can see occurring in some [664] of those who have become drunk. When they are comatose, they both stagger and are heavy in the head, and are unable to part their eyelids even if they were to force themselves; yet, on the other hand, through this very heaviness they are unable to sleep, being in distress, turning about, throwing themselves around, and changing now to one posture, now to another. For one can often see these things occurring in those who have become drunk, when their head has been filled during the drunkenness and then, by its heaviness, at the same time drags them down into coma and prevents sleep. So also, then, when an unconcocted plethora has taken hold of the head, they are at once sleepless and comatose. And when this arises at the beginning, sometimes it is concocted well, just as would also be the case if such a condition had arisen from wine; but in this case neither lethargus nor phrenitis followed. Sometimes, however, having become acrid, it ended in phrenitis. In many, again, the thinner part, having been either concocted or dispersed, was evacuated, while the thicker part remained, and in this way lethargus supervened.

RENDERING 2

For something similar happens to people at the beginning of many diseases, such as one may see occurring in some [664] of those who have become drunk. When they are comatose, they both stagger and are heavy in the head, and cannot raise their eyelids even if they force themselves; yet on account of this very heaviness, in turn, they are unable to sleep, being in distress, tossing about, throwing themselves this way and that, and changing now into one posture, now into another. For these things can often be seen occurring in those who have become drunk, their head having been filled in the drunkenness and then, by its heaviness, at once dragging them down into coma and at once preventing sleep. So too, then, when an unconcocted plenitude has taken hold of the head, they are at the same time both sleepless and comatose. And when this occurs at the beginning, sometimes it is well concocted, just as such a thing would have come about from wine as well; but in that case neither lethargy nor phrenitis followed; sometimes, however, it became acrid and ended in phrenitis. In many, again, the thinner part was evacuated, either by being concocted or by being dispersed, while the thicker part remained, and thus lethargy supervened.

When such a disease has advanced, such a condition is, as we have shown, already close to phrenitis; but as those who introduce the mixed affection compounded of lethargus [665] and phrenitis suppose, it would seem to be more akin to that. μεμνήσῃ πρῶτον μὲν τῆς διαιρέσεως, ἂν μὴ μέλλῃς τὰ παρόντα [656] παρακούειν, ὅταν καταφορὰν τὴν μὲν ὑπνώδη, τὴν δὲ ἄγρυπνον ἐκάλουν, καὶ τὴν μὲν ὑπνώδη ἰδίαν εἶναι τῶν ληθαργικῶν καὶ διαπαντὸς ὑπάρχειν αὐτοῖς, τὴν δὲ οὔτε ἰδίαν εἶναι τῶν φρενιτικῶν οὔτε ὑπάρχειν αὐτοῖς διαπαντός. RENDERING 1

You will remember, first of all, the division—if you are not going to misunderstand what is presently [656] being said—when I called one kind of catophora sleepy and the other sleepless, and (said) that the sleepy kind is proper to lethargics and is present in them throughout, whereas the other is neither proper to phrenitics nor present in them throughout. The sleepy kind, then, so far as concerns the present matter, befalls lethargics; the sleepless kind, which supervenes in phrenitics, I shall try to delimit, so that you may be able to discover the difference spoken of by Hippocrates. For this man was not one of those who apply names at random, nor is the word 'sluggish' added to catophora thoughtlessly, nor does he say, as Homer does, 'liquid oil' and 'white milk' for the sake of no distinction at all—for indeed all milk is white and all oil liquid—but with him every word and every syllable signifies some real thing.

RENDERING 2

You will remember, first, the division, if you are not going to mishear what is now being said [656]: I called one kind of kataphora sleepy and the other wakeful, and said that the sleepy kind is proper to lethargics and always present in them, while the other is neither proper to phrenitics nor always present in them. The sleepy kind, then, as far as the present matter goes, occurs in lethargics. The wakeful kind, which supervenes in phrenitics, I shall try to define, so that you may be able to find the difference stated by Hippocrates. For this man was not one of those who apply names idly. The word 'torpid' is not attached to 'kataphora' senselessly, nor does he say, as Homer does, 'liquid oil' and 'white milk' for the sake of no distinction at all, for all milk is white and all oil liquid. With him, rather, every word and every syllable signifies some thing.

ἐν πάσαις γὰρ ταῖς εἰρημέναις ῥήσεσιν [645] ἀγρυπνίας καὶ κώματος φαίνεται μνημονεύων. ἕτερον δ’ ἄῤῥωστον αὖθις γράφων ἐν τῷ αὐτῷ βιβλίῳ φησί· τουτέῳ διὰ τέλεος, ἐξ οὗ καὶ ἐγὼ οἶδα, κοιλίη ταραχώδης, οὖρα λεπτὰ μέλανα, κωματώδης, ἄγρυπνος. ὥστ’ ἄντικρυς εἶναι δῆλον, ὅτι μηδ’ ὕπνον, μηδὲ καταφορὰν παρ’ αὐτῷ δηλοῖ τὸ τοῦ κώματος ὄνομα. διὰ τοῦτο, οἶμαι, τινὲς τῶν ἐξηγητῶν ἀπὸ τοῦ κωμάζειν φασὶ τοὺς κωματώδεις ὠνομάσθαι· ἐγρηγορέναι μὲν γὰρ καὶ τοὺς κωμάζοντας, ἐγρηγορέναι δὲ καὶ τοὺς κωματώδεις. καὶ νὴ Δία ὥσπερ οἱ κωμάζοντες οὐ πάνυ τι κατανεύουσιν, οὕτως οὐδὲ τοὺς κωματίζοντας, καὶ μάλιστα τοῖν ὀφθαλμοῖν. ὑγρούς τε γὰρ εἶναι καὶ ἐρυθροὺς καὶ ἀστηρίκτους καὶ ἐνδεδυμένους· ἀλλὰ τὸ πᾶν ἀμφοτέροις σῶμα σφαλερὸν ἐν ταῖς κινήσεσι καὶ ἀκρατές· αὐτὸν διὰ τοῦτο καὶ τὸν Ἱπποκράτην περὶ τῆς ὅλης συνδρομῆς ἀπορεῖν, εἴτε χρὴ φρενιτικοὺς αὐτοὺς ὀνομάζειν, εἴτ’ ἄλλο τι.

RENDERING 1

For in all the passages cited [645] he is evidently making mention of sleeplessness and coma. And again, writing of another patient in the same book, he says: "In this man throughout, from the time when I too had knowledge of him, the bowels were disturbed, urine thin and black; comatose, sleepless." So that it is plainly evident that in him the name of coma signifies neither sleep nor kataphora. For this reason, I think, some of the commentators say that the comatose are named from revelling (kōmazein); for those who revel are awake, and the comatose are awake as well. And, by Zeus, just as revellers do not altogether nod downward, so neither do those in a state of coma (κωματίζοντας), and especially as regards the two eyes: for these are moist and red and unsteady and sunken (ἐνδεδυμένους); but the whole body in both is unsteady in its movements and without control; and that for this reason Hippocrates himself is at a loss concerning the whole concurrence of symptoms, whether one ought to call them phrenitic or something else.

RENDERING 2

For in all the passages quoted he evidently mentions [645] both sleeplessness and coma. And again, writing of another patient in the same book, he says: 'In this man throughout, from the time that I too knew him, the bowel was disturbed, urine thin, black; he was comatose, sleepless.' So it is plainly evident that with him the name of coma signifies neither sleep nor torpor (kataphora). For this reason, I suppose, some of the exegetes say that the comatose are named from 'revelling' (kōmazein). For revellers are awake, and the comatose are awake too. And, by Zeus, just as revellers do not nod down at all, so neither do those in a coma, and especially not with their eyes. For their eyes are moist and red and unsteady and sunken. But in both the whole body is unsteady in its movements and lacking in control. For this reason, they say, Hippocrates himself is also at a loss about the whole syndrome, whether one should call these patients phrenitics or something else.

Let me say, so that what is being said may become clear also to those less [661] trained: the 'ἆρά γε, is it day?' and 'ἆρά γε, will he come today?' contain a dubitative adverb. But if I wish to make a declaration to them in speech, I do so either by simply saying thus, without any demonstration, 'he will come today', or with demonstration, thus: 'if the sun is above the earth, it is day'; 'if he walks, therefore (ἄρα) he moves'. Now when we declare something without demonstration, we have no need of the adverb ἄρα, for the expression is superfluous, and no one says thus, 'it is day, therefore (ἄρα)', nor thus, 'ἆρά γε, it is day', when it is possible to say simply thus, 'it is day'.
