ἐκπίπτοντα [442] γὰρ τοῦ κατὰ φύσιν, ἐν ἀκαρεῖ μέν τινι χρόνῳ τὴν γένεσιν ἔχει, τοὐντεῦθεν δὲ ἀναβαίνει μέχρι τῆς ἀκμῆς. ἔστω γὰρ εἰ τύχοι κατὰ δυσκρασίαν τὸ νόσημα, καθ’ ἣν ἡμῖν ἐπιδέδεικται καὶ ὁ πυρετὸς συνιστάμενος. ἀναγκαῖον οὖν ἐπειδὰν πρῶτον ἡ παρὰ φύσιν αὕτη γένηται θερμασία, πυρέττειν τε τὸν ἄνθρωπον, ἐπιτείνεσθαί τε μέχρι τῆς ἀκμῆς τὴν θερμασίαν. οὗ γὰρ οὐκ ἔστι μέρη, πῶς ἂν εἴη γένεσις ἐν χρόνῳ τοῦδε; ταῦτ’ εἴρηταί μοι καὶ κατ’ ἀρχὰς εὐθέως, ἀπήντησά τε πρὸς αὐτὰ, τῶν μετὰ περιψύξεως εἰσβαλλόντων πυρετῶν ἀναμνήσας. ἀλλ’ ἐν μὲν ταῖς περιόδοις αὐτάρκης ἡ ἀπάντησις· εἷς γὰρ τῶν ἐν μέρει καιρῶν καὶ τῇ τάξει πρῶτος ὁ τῆς περιψύξεως τῆς συμπάσης νόσου· γελοιότατον δ’ ἂν εἴη τὸν τοῦ πρώτου παροξυσμοῦ πρῶτον καιρὸν ἀρχὴν ἡγεῖσθαι τοῦ νοσήματος ὅλου. τὸ γάρ τοι χρήσιμον ὁ καιρὸς οὗτος, ὃν ὀνομάζουσιν ἀρχὴν νοσήματος, εἰς τὰ βοηθήματα κέκτηται, καὶ πρὸς τοῦτον ἀποβλέπων εἶπεν· ἀρχομένων τῶν νούσων, ἤν τι δοκέῃ κινέειν, κίνει, ἀκμαζουσῶν δὲ, ἡσυχίαν ἔχειν βέλτιόν ἐστιν.

RENDERING 1

For falling away [442] from the natural state, they have their coming-to-be in some instant of time, and from there they ascend up to the acme. For let the disease be, say, one consisting in a dyscrasia, in accordance with which, as has been demonstrated by us, fever too is constituted. It is necessary, then, that as soon as this heat contrary to nature first arises, the man is in fever, and that the heat is intensified up to the acme. For of that which has no parts, how could there be a coming-to-be in time? These things were stated by me right at the beginning too, and I replied to them, by recalling the fevers that make their attack with chilling. But in the case of the periods the reply is sufficient; for the time of the chilling is one of the partial times of the whole disease and first in order; but it would be most ridiculous to regard the first time of the first paroxysm as the beginning of the whole disease. For indeed this time, which they name the beginning of the disease, possesses its usefulness with regard to remedies, and looking to this he said: "When diseases are beginning, if anything seems to need moving, move it; but when they are at their acme, it is better to keep quiet."

RENDERING 2

For, falling away [442] from the natural state, they have their coming-into-being in an instant of time, and from there they ascend up to the peak. For let the disease be, say, one consisting in a dyscrasia, the condition in which we have shown fever too to be constituted. It is necessary, then, that as soon as this unnatural heat first arises, the person is feverish, and that the heat is intensified up to the peak. 'For of that which has no parts, how could there be a coming-into-being in time?' These things were said by me right at the beginning as well, and I answered them by recalling the fevers that set in with chilling. But in the case of the periods the answer is sufficient; for the time of the chilling is one of the partial times of the whole disease, and the first in order. It would, however, be most ridiculous to regard the first time of the first paroxysm as the beginning of the whole disease. For this time, which they call the beginning of a disease, has its usefulness with respect to the remedies, and it was looking to this that he said: 'When diseases are beginning, if anything seems to need moving, move it; but when they are at their peak, it is better to keep quiet.'

RENDERING 1

For as soon as the decline first becomes clear to perception, we then use the remedies suited to it; [449] but so long as the magnitude of the fever stands at the same numbers, for so long it is proper to do what belongs to the magnitude of the peak. In the same way, in ophthalmia, pleuritis, every inflammation and each of the other diseases, the general periods, measured by perception, dictate the use of the different remedies. The inquiry into the breadth of the peak is therefore a logical one, whether one is considering something about the particular paroxysms or about the whole disease. But it is not my present purpose to distinguish such matters, as those do who bring everything into everything, among whom is Archigenes. For this man mixes into the most strictly medical treatises sometimes an inquiry about names, and at times also one about the things themselves, but a logical one. We, just as we have always guarded against this, shall do so now as well. Leaving it to others to inquire about the peak, whether it is timeless or occupies some time, we shall measure its use. For we see its nature plainly: among the ages of life, the age of prime runs its course over a broad span of time, and in diseases the peak runs its course sometimes over a longer span, often over a shorter one, but in every case over a perceptible one.

RENDERING 2

For when the decline first becomes clear to perception, then we employ the remedies that suit it; [449] but so long as the magnitude of the fever stands at equal measures (ἀπὸ τῶν ἴσων ἀριθμῶν), for so long it is fitting to do what is proper to the magnitude of the acme. In the same way, in ophthalmia and pleuritis and every inflammation and each of the other diseases, the general periods dictate the use of the different remedies, being measured by perception. The inquiry about the breadth of the acme, then, becomes a logical one, whether one were to examine it with regard to the particular paroxysms or with regard to the whole disease; but at present it is not my purpose to draw distinctions about such matters, as those do who bring everything into everything, one of whom is Archigenes. For this man mixes into the most strictly medical treatises sometimes an inquiry about names, and sometimes one about the things themselves, but a logical one; whereas we, just as we have always guarded against this, shall act in the same way now too: leaving it to others to inquire about the acme, whether it is without duration or with some duration, we shall measure its usefulness, seeing clearly that the acmastic nature, both in the ages of life, is dissolved over a broad span of time, and in diseases sometimes over a longer, often over a shorter, but in any case over a perceptible one.

RENDERING 1

Their nature is twofold. For some of them have the constitution [sc. of fatal diseases?] [455] whenever they have it brought exactly to completion; others are of the nature of those that are resolved, but when [the disease] kills first, it is in this respect that this happens to them. And of these too the manner is threefold: for they carry off [the patient] before being resolved either because of the patient's want of strength, or because of the strength of the diseases, or because of both together. But on another account there is one common pattern for all such cases, namely when [the disease] is beyond the strength. For the very cause that sustains the patient's destruction is set in this: no one can be saved unless nature masters the disease, nor perish without nature being defeated and the disease becoming more powerful than it. And for this reason some think that a single thing is signified by 'great disease', as though it consisted in a relation to something. They do not know that 'great disease' is sometimes said in this way, just as if we were also to say 'greater', when it is beyond the strength. But it is also said with reference to its own proper substance, as a man, or a horse, or an ox is called great.

RENDERING 2

RENDERING 1

In the same way he has also said this: [443] 'About the beginnings and the ends all things are weaker.' And moreover there is also what is said about purgation, that one should purge and set in motion what is concocted, not what is raw, nor at the beginnings, unless there is turgescence. And in addition to these there is further the saying, 'In acute affections use purgative medicines rarely and at the beginnings.' These, and many other things besides, said some by Hippocrates and others by all the other ancient physicians, do not permit us to understand the beginning as the onset of the first paroxysm. They require us to take it as extending over a longer span, long enough that one can perform phlebotomy and use a clyster, and sometimes, if it is urgent, also purge. Besides, it would be ridiculous to posit an equal beginning for diseases that are resolved within the seventh day and for those that persist for a year, as the quartan sometimes does. Much, then, has been said by me about this beginning also in the first book On Crises, where I showed how one might recognise this first period. Its chief point was the unconcoctedness of the disease. But it is better here too to say, in addition to those things, whatever is useful for the present exposition.

Τούτων ἡμῖν αὐτάρκως ἀποδεικνυμένων, ἐπισκεψόμεθα περὶ τῶν μὴ λυομένων νοσημάτων.

RENDERING 1

Each of the diseases has its own proper period of origination, sometimes perceptible, sometimes not perceptible; after which, in turn, the period of increase is not perceptible in the same way in [454] all cases, not even this one. These are followed by the period of the peak, which is in every case perceptible, although we grant that sometimes there is no clear mark of the beginning of the affection and of its increase. In these cases, then, so far as the use of the art is concerned, one will truly say that only the time of the peak exists; and, if the patients are going to be saved, that the period of the decline necessarily follows it, and this too is perceptible, since it is constituted in a time that is not without extension. For even if someone has an acute crisis, whether through a haemorrhage or through sweats, there is at any rate some period of decline in this case too, and a perceptible one. And if in other diseases this occurs to a lesser degree, the disease declining over three or four or even more days, this plainly does no harm to the preceding argument, nor does it discredit the chief point of the assertion; rather, it still stands that all diseases that are resolved have, just as a peak, so also some decline. These things having been sufficiently demonstrated by us, we shall next examine the diseases that are not resolved.

RENDERING 2
