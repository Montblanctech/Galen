Galen Density Project

Galen in English for the first time

Galen of Pergamon was a doctor in second-century Rome, and more of his writing survives than of any other ancient Greek author. Much of it has never been translated into English. Here are nine of those works, put into English by machine: rough, unchecked by any scholar, and readable today.

Why nobody had done it

Galen's words fall through the dictionary

Galen wrote in a specialist Greek full of drug names, anatomy and rare technical words. The standard Greek–English dictionary (LSJ) covers many of those words thinly or not at all. That gap makes him hard to translate, for people and for machines: earlier research found that the rarer a passage's terminology, the worse machine translation of Galen does.

From the project's list of 1,433 words that are distinctive to Galen and thinly glossed, checked by hand. Look any word up in the Term Explorer.

Method

How they were made

  1. The GreekThe text comes from Kühn's 1820s edition, as digitised by the First1KGreek project, and is shown beside every passage.
  2. Two translationsTwo different language models translated each passage separately, with the same plain instructions and no help.
  3. Three checksEach passage is translated back into Greek, the two versions are compared term by term, and a model judge tests the technical terms against the dictionary.
  4. Failures keptNothing is hidden or smoothed over. Passages that fail the checks stay on the page, marked, so a reader knows where to be careful.

Found a mistake? Report a correction; that is what these drafts are for.
